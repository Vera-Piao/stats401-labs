Dataset Description

2025 Nominal GDP

This dataset records 2025 nominal GDP for the world’s 50 largest economies. GDP is measured in billions of current U.S. dollars. The data are based on the IMF October 2025 World Economic Outlook.

Choropleth Map

GDP Encoded with Color

Darker blue represents higher 2025 nominal GDP. A logarithmic color scale keeps differences among smaller and medium-sized economies visible despite the strongly skewed distribution.

Scroll to zoom and drag to pan. Hover over a country to inspect it in both views.

Cartogram

GDP Encoded with Area

This Dorling cartogram replaces country shapes with circles near their geographic positions. Circle area represents GDP, so larger economies occupy more visual area; circles move slightly to avoid overlap.

Design and Comparison

Choropleth and Cartogram Trade-offs

The choropleth makes the United States and China most prominent because they receive the darkest colors, while Germany, Japan, and India form the next visible tier. However, geographic area remains unchanged, so large countries such as Russia and Canada occupy substantial space even when their GDP is far below the two leaders, and small economies can be difficult to see. The Dorling cartogram changes that emphasis: the United States and China become the largest circles; Germany, Japan, India, and the United Kingdom expand relative to their land area; and geographically large but lower-GDP countries shrink. The choropleth preserves recognizable boundaries, coastlines, relative position, and adjacency, making it better for locating countries and studying regional patterns. Its logarithmic color scale also reveals differences across the highly skewed GDP distribution, although color is less direct for comparing exact magnitudes. The cartogram represents GDP through circle area, making magnitude rankings and large-versus-small comparisons more immediate. In exchange, it replaces country shapes and moves circles to avoid overlap, so exact borders, area, and some neighborhood relationships are lost. Moving from color to area makes global economic concentration feel more dramatic: a small number of economies dominate the display, while many familiar large land areas become visually secondary. The views therefore support different tasks and work best together through linked highlighting.
