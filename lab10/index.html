How to use

  1. Open on localhost or HTTPS in a browser with speech recognition (Chrome is a practical choice). Press Start voice control and allow microphone access.
  2. Say “Highlight Japan”, “Select China”, “Clear selection”, or “Reset”. Each start listens for one utterance. Press Start again for another command.
  3. Check the transcript and action below. Unsupported speech keeps the current view and gives guidance.

Country names come from the 50-economy dataset; “USA”, “US”, “UK”, and “South Korea” are also accepted. Selection stays visible in both maps, including GDP and rank. Reset clears selection and restores map zoom.

Fallback: choose a country above, type a command, or click either map. Tab to a country and press Enter or Space to select. All buttons and form controls also work by keyboard.

Privacy: this page never starts the microphone automatically and does not store transcripts or recordings. Your browser’s speech service may send audio to its provider for processing. No camera is used. Stop listening cancels recognition; leaving the page also cancels it. You can use all fallback controls without granting microphone permission.

Design Reflection · 189 words

Speaking to a linked map

I extended my Lab 9 geospatial visualization, retaining its GDP choropleth, Dorling cartogram, logarithmic color scale, and linked country selection. The new modality is English voice control through the browser’s Web Speech API. Saying “Highlight Japan” or “Select China” selects the same economy in both views and displays its GDP and rank. “Clear selection” removes the highlight, while “Reset” also restores the map zoom. These commands support locating a named economy and comparing its geographic footprint with its economic importance without searching for a small map shape. Voice is appropriate for categorical selection because country names express the intended target directly. A visible transcript and action message make interpretation inspectable, and a country menu, typed commands, mouse, and keyboard provide alternatives. Exact command matching avoids accidental actions from unrelated speech. Testing showed that the one-command listening cycle needs clear instructions: users must wait for the listening indicator and restart recognition before each new utterance. Commands must also follow the supported selection syntax. These constraints can interrupt exploration, so visible feedback and conventional controls remain essential. Microphone access begins only after an explicit button press, and users can stop listening.
